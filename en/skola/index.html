C

8 CARDS · ~5 MIN

Wall

  1. 08

    Frame — stud 5/⁠15, spacing 60

    Dry timber studs at 60 cm spacing.

    STAGE 3/⁠10 · WALL FRAME AND DIAGONALS

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Stud 5/⁠15 · spacing 60 cm
    WHAT IT IS
    Dry timber studs at 60 cm spacing.
    WHAT IT DOES
    The wall's load-bearing skeleton: every layer and the roof stand on it.
    WITHOUT IT
    With spacing over 60, the wool cannot sit tight and the wall weakens.
    CHECK ON SITE
    Measure the spacing with a tape; studs 5/⁠15.

    EXPLANATION

    The wall starts with a skeleton: dry timber studs, 5/⁠15 cm, 60 cm apart. Everything stands on these studs — the wool, the membrane, the roof. The spacing is 60 cm so that the wool fits tightly between the studs; with wider spacing it cannot sit tight, and the wall weakens too.

    Next card: Diagonals — at both ends of the wall↓

  2. 09

    Diagonals — at both ends of the wall

    Two diagonals on every wall — one at each end, sloping opposite ways.

    STAGE 3/⁠10 · WALL FRAME AND DIAGONALS

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Two diagonals on every wall — one at each end, sloping opposite ways
    WHAT IT IS
    Two diagonals on every wall — one at each end, sloping opposite ways.
    WHAT IT DOES
    Keeps the frame from leaning sideways, to the left and to the right — the wall's stiffness comes from them.
    WITHOUT IT
    The frame leans into a parallelogram.
    CHECK ON SITE
    On every wall, two diagonals — one at each end, sloping opposite ways.

    EXPLANATION

    A rectangular frame is not stiff on its own: push it from the side and it turns into a parallelogram. A sloped member between the studs prevents exactly that — the wall locks into triangles and no longer leans. That is why we fit two diagonals on every wall — one at each end, sloping opposite ways — so the wall stands up to a push from the left and from the right.

    Next card: Horizontal noggins↓

  3. 10

    Horizontal noggins

    Short pieces set between the studs, staggered.

    STAGE 3/⁠10 · WALL FRAME AND DIAGONALS

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Horizontal noggins — between the studs
    WHAT IT IS
    Short pieces set between the studs, staggered.
    WHAT IT DOES
    Tie the studs together and keep a long stud from bending.
    WITHOUT IT
    Each stud works on its own.
    CHECK ON SITE
    Noggins between the studs, at different heights in neighbouring bays.

    EXPLANATION

    A long stud standing alone tends to bend. Short horizontal noggins between the studs tie them to each other — so it is no longer each stud on its own, but the whole wall together. In neighbouring bays the noggins sit at different heights — staggered.

    Next card: Wind membrane — outside↓

  4. 11

    Wind membrane — outside

    A breathable layer on the frame, under the facade.

    STAGE 5/⁠10 · MEMBRANE AND BATTENS

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Wind membrane — outside, on the frame
    WHAT IT IS
    A breathable layer on the frame, under the facade.
    WHAT IT DOES
    Lets vapour out, and keeps wind and damp from getting in.
    WITHOUT IT
    The wool gets wet — with a membrane turned the wrong way, too.
    CHECK ON SITE
    Membrane under the battens, seams lapped top over bottom.

    EXPLANATION

    On the outer side of the frame sits the membrane — a thin layer, but a clever one: it lets the vapour coming from the rooms out, and keeps wind and rain damp from getting in. Its direction matters: a membrane turned the wrong way wets the wool. The seams overlap top over bottom, like roof tiles, so water cannot run in.

    Next card: Battens — 25 mm air gap↓

  5. 12

    Battens — 25 mm air gap

    Vertical battens on the membrane.

    STAGE 5/⁠10 · MEMBRANE AND BATTENS

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Air gap 25 mm · battens · bottom edge — mesh
    WHAT IT IS
    Vertical battens on the membrane.
    WHAT IT DOES
    Make an air channel behind the facade — damp goes up and out.
    WITHOUT IT
    With no gap, or the bottom edge closed, the facade rots from behind.
    CHECK ON SITE
    Gap 25 mm, bottom edge open (mesh), top edge open.

    EXPLANATION

    Behind the facade panel, 25 mm of air moves — in at the bottom, out at the top. After rain the panel dries from both sides and rot does not start. The bottom edge has a mesh: insects and mice cannot get in, while the air passes freely.

    Next card: Facade panel 13 mm↓

  6. 13

    Facade panel 13 mm

    The facade panel, on the battens.

    STAGE 7/⁠10 · FACADE

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Facade panel 13 mm — on battens
    WHAT IT IS
    The facade panel, on the battens.
    WHAT IT DOES
    Shields from the weather and gives the house its face.
    WITHOUT IT
    Nailed straight onto the membrane, the facade has no air gap.
    CHECK ON SITE
    The panel is on battens, not on the membrane.

    EXPLANATION

    The 13 mm facade panel is fixed to the battens, not to the membrane — it is the batten that leaves the air gap behind it. The panel shields from the weather and gives the house its face, but how long it lasts depends on what is behind it: a facade nailed straight onto the membrane cannot dry.

    Next card: Stone wool in the wall — 3 × 50 = 150 mm↓

  7. 14

    Stone wool in the wall — 3 × 50 = 150 mm

    Three layers between the studs, 50 kg/⁠m³.

    STAGE 9/⁠10 · INSULATION AND VAPOUR BARRIER

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Stone wool 3 × 50 = 150 mm · 50 kg/⁠m³
    WHAT IT IS
    Three layers between the studs, 50 kg/⁠m³.
    WHAT IT DOES
    Keeps the warmth inside.
    WITHOUT IT
    With one layer, or thin wool, the wall is cold.
    CHECK ON SITE
    Three layers, no gaps, to the full stud depth.

    EXPLANATION

    Between the studs is stone wool — three 50 mm layers, 150 mm in all, density 50 kg/⁠m³. It is this layer that keeps the warmth in. Not only the thickness matters, but how it lies: to the full stud depth, with no gaps — every gap is a cold bridge. One layer, or a thin one, and the wall is cold.

    Next card: Vapour membrane — inside↓

  8. 15

    Vapour membrane — inside

    A barrier over the wool, on the warm side.

    STAGE 9/⁠10 · INSULATION AND VAPOUR BARRIER

    THIS IS HOW WE BUILD
    Vapour membrane — inside, on the warm side
    WHAT IT IS
    A barrier over the wool, on the warm side.
    WHAT IT DOES
    Keeps room vapour out of the wall, so the wool stays dry.
    WITHOUT IT
    In winter, condensation forms in the wool.
    CHECK ON SITE
    Before the interior panel goes on: seams taped.

    EXPLANATION

    Room air is warm and humid — breathing, cooking, the shower. That vapour pushes towards the wall. The vapour membrane lies over the wool on the warm side and keeps the vapour out of the wall, so the wool stays dry. Without it, condensation forms in the wool in winter. We tape the seams: with an open seam, the barrier is no longer a barrier.
